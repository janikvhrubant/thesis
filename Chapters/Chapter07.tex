\chapter{Empirical Evaluation and Discussion}
\label{chapter7}

In the last chapter, the Koksma--Hlawka inequality was applied to the function
approximation problem via \acfp{dnn}. These theoretical results, yielded a
significant improvement in the approximation of the generalization error for
training sets sampled from low-discrepancy sequences compared to pseudo-random
sampling.

In this chapter, these theoretical results are empirically evaluated for
different examples.

\section{Implementation details of the DNN surrogate training}
\label{sec:impl_details_dnn}

Before applying the Deep Learning algorithm to optimize the weights $\theta$ of
the \aclp{dnn} for the approximation different of different ground truth
functions $f$ later in this chapter, some implementation details are provided in
the following. The ground truth functions studied in this chapter are the smooth
sum of sines function on multiple dimensions and the approximate \acf{ode}
solution to the distance achieved by projectile motion. The implementation is
done in Python using the \emph{PyTorch} library \cite{paszke2019pytorch} and
accessible via
GitHub\footnote{\url{https://github.com/janikvhrubant/nn-sampling-analysis}}.

As the main goal of this chapter is to evaluate the impact of different sampling
strategies, the Deep Learning algorithm was applied on training sets sampled
from three different strategies: pseudo-random sampling and two low-discrepancy
sequences: Sobol' and Halton as introduced in Chapter~\ref{chapter3}. Each
training set is obtained by evaluating the ground truth function $f$ at the
sampled points. In the experiments, the number of training samples $N$ is varied
from $64$ up to $8192$. According to the training set, a test set $\mathcal{T}$
is chosen from the remaining sampling strategies. To ensure comparability and
uniform coverage of the domain, the test sets are always chosen to be the full
low-discrepancy sequence with $|\mathcal{T}|=8192$ points. The setup can be
summarized as in Table~\ref{tab:test_set}.

\section{Multi-dimensional sum of sines}

As a first example, the multidimensional and nonlinear sum of sines function is studied in for different dimensions $s\in\{6,8,10\}$
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sum_of_sines}
    f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{s} \sin( 4\pi x_i), \quad \mathbf{x} \in [0,1]^s.
\end{equation}
To add variance to the function, the input is scaled by a factor of $4\pi$. It
is clear, that $f\in C^\infty([0,1]^s)$ and thus
$V_\mathrm{HK}(f)\sim\hat{V}_\mathrm{HK}(f)<\infty$ on the compact domain
$[0,1]^s$.

\section{Projectile Motion}
As a second illustrative example, this section investigates the motion of a
projectile. The experiment is introduced by formulating the underlying \acp{ode}
system that describes the physical dynamics of the process. Based on approximate
solutions of these \acp{ode} at the prescribed training points, \acp{dnn} are
trained and subsequently evaluated.

\subsection{ODE formulation of projectile motion}
\label{subsec:proj_ode}
\emph{Projectile motion} describes the trajectory of an object launched into the
air and evolving under gravity and aerodynamic drag. We consider a planar motion
with state
\begin{equation*}
x(t)\in\mathbb{R} \quad (\text{horizontal position}),\qquad
h(t)\in\mathbb{R} \quad (\text{vertical position}),
\end{equation*}
and velocities $u(t)=\dot{x}(t)$, $w(t)=\dot{h}(t)$, according to the system
parameters $\theta$ (defined below), including the launch angle $\alpha$. The
motion is terminated at the first impact time
\begin{equation*}
t_\star \;=\; \inf\{\, t>0 \;:\; h(t)=0 \,\},
\end{equation*}
and the traveled range is 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:proj_range}
R(\theta) \;=\; x(t_\star).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Parameters and modeling assumptions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The moving object is modeled as a spherical projectile with mass $m$
    in the range $0.3 - 0.78\,\mathrm{kg}$ and radius $r$ in range $0.0213 -
    0.124 \,\mathrm{m}$. The extreme values correspond to a golf ball of gold
    and a basketball, respectively.
    \item Gravitational acceleration is constant, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$.
    \item Air density $\rho$ is varying in the range of $0.269 -
    1.225\,\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m}^3}$ which corresponds to the air
    density at the Mount Everest summit and at sea level, respectively.
    \item The drag coefficient $c_d$ of the projectile is constant in the range
    $0.25 - 0.7$.
    \item The initial hight $h_0$ of the projectile is in the range $1 -
    100\,\mathrm{m}$.
    \item The initial speed $v_0$ of the projectile is in the range $1-50
    \mathrm{m/s}$.
    \item The launch angle $\alpha$ of the projectile is in the range $0 -
    90^\circ$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Quadratic-drag model}\ \\
Let $\mathbf{z}(t) = (x(t),h(t),u(t),w(t))^\top$ with horizontal/vertical
positions $(x,h)$ and velocities $(u,w)=(\dot x,\dot h)$. Denote $\mathbf
v=(u,w)$ and $\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{u^2+w^2}$. For a spherical body of radius $r$
and mass $m$ in air of density $\rho$ with constant drag coefficient $c_d$, the
cross-sectional area is $A=\pi r^2$ and the drag-specific constant is

\begin{equation*}
k \;=\; \frac{\rho\,c_d\,A}{2m}\;=\;\frac{\rho\,c_d\,\pi r^2}{2m}.
\end{equation*}

The physically consistent planar model with quadratic aerodynamic drag reads

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:proj_ode_vector}
\dot{\mathbf z}(t)
\;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
u\\
w\\
-\,k\,\|\mathbf v\|\,u\\
-\,g \;-\; k\,\|\mathbf v\|\,w
\end{pmatrix}\,.
\end{equation}

Equivalently, in component form,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:proj_ode_components}
\dot x = u,\quad
\dot h = w,\quad
\dot u = -\,k\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\,u,\quad
\dot w = -\,g - k\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\,w.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Initial conditions} \ \\
Given launch speed $v_0>0$, angle $\alpha\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]$ and height
$h_0\ge 0$,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:proj_ic}
x(0)=0,\qquad h(0)=h_0,\qquad
u(0)=v_0\cos\alpha,\qquad w(0)=v_0\sin\alpha.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Parameterization for learning} \ \\
We collect the physical parameters in

\begin{equation*}
    \theta \;=\; (\rho, r, c_d, m, h_0, \alpha, v_0) \in \Theta
\end{equation*}

with the given application-driven bounds:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
&\rho\in[\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}] = [0.269,\,1.225]~\mathrm{kg/m^3},\quad
r\in[r_{\min},r_{\max}] = [0.0213,\,0.124]~\mathrm{m},\\
&c_d\in[c_{d,\min},c_{d,\max}] = [0.25,\,0.70],\quad
m\in[m_{\min},m_{\max}] = [0.30,\,0.78]~\mathrm{kg},\\
&h_0\in[h_{0,\min},h_{0,\max}] = [1,\,100]~\mathrm{m},\quad
\alpha\in[0,\,\frac{\pi}{2}],\quad
v_0\in[v_{0,\min},v_{0,\max}] = [1,\,50]~\mathrm{m/s}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

Here, $\Theta$ denotes the Cartesian product of all admissible intervals from
above. In the experiments, $\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^7$ is sampled according to the
chosen sampling method. Then, $\theta(\mathbf y)$ is mapped affinely to the
physical parameter ranges via

\begin{equation*}
p(\mathbf y)=p_{\min}+(p_{\max}-p_{\min})\,y,\quad
p\in\{\rho,r,c_d,m,h_0,\alpha,v_0\},
\end{equation*}

and define the parameter-to-output map

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:param_to_output}
\mathcal{F}:\ [0,1]^7\to\mathbb{R},\qquad
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf y)\;=\;R\bigl(\theta(\mathbf y)\bigr),
\end{equation}

where $R(\cdot)$ is given by \eqref{eq:proj_range} for the ODE
\eqref{eq:proj_ode_vector}-\eqref{eq:proj_ic}. The \acp{dnn} are trained to
approximate $\mathcal{F}$.

\paragraph{Well-posedness} \ \\
The right-hand side in \eqref{eq:proj_ode_vector} is continuous and locally
Lipschitz in $\mathbf z$ (the map $\mathbf v\mapsto \|\mathbf v\|\,\mathbf v$ is
locally Lipschitz), hence for any fixed $\theta\in\Theta$ there exists a unique
maximal solution up to $t_\star$. Moreover $h(t)$ is strictly decreasing after
the apex, ensuring a unique impact time.

\paragraph{Numerical solution} \ \\
We integrate \eqref{eq:proj_ode_vector} with a fixed-step explicit scheme. By a
small step size $\Delta t>0$, updates are computed with the forward Euler
method

\begin{equation*}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_{n+1}\\
h_{n+1}\\
u_{n+1}\\
w_{n+1}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_n\\
h_n\\
u_n\\
w_n
\end{pmatrix}
+
\Delta t
\begin{pmatrix}
u_n\\
w_n\\
-\,k\,\|\mathbf{v}_n\|\,u_n\\
-\,g - k\,\|\mathbf{v}_n\|\,w_n
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}

and terminate at the first $n$ with $h_{n+1}\le 0$. This approximates the traveled range $R \approx x_{n+1}$.
